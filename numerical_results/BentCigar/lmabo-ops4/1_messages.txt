   - **UCB and TS** were heavily favored to explore high-uncertainty regions, but no improvement occurred. This suggests the optimizer was **exploring "useless" regions** far from the minimum, where the function’s values explode.
   - The **shortest distance** between points sometimes approached 1.0, indicating exploration of distant areas, but these regions were unproductive.
---
### Why an Exponential Function?
- **Extreme Range**: An exponential function like \( e^{\|\mathbf{x}\|^2} \) naturally produces values that grow rapidly away from the origin (or another point), matching the observed \( f_{\text{range}} \).
- **Narrow Minimum**: The minimum would be a **sharp spike** (e.g., at \(\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}\)), making it easy to find early but hard to improve upon.
- **GP Behavior**: The Matérn 5/2 kernel with ARD would struggle to model the abrupt changes in such a function, leading to persistent uncertainty (high outputscale) and erratic lengthscales.
---
### Alternative Possibilities:
- **Quadratic Function with Large Coefficients**: A scaled quadratic function (e.g., \( f(\mathbf{x}) = 10^6 \cdot \|\mathbf{x}\|^2 + 8,022,951 \)) could also produce a large range, but the exponential’s faster growth better explains the 2.6 billion maximum.
- **Noisy Constant Function**: If the function were nearly constant except at the minimum, noise could explain the high \( f_{\text{range}} \). However, the consistent \( f_{\text{min}} \) suggests a true global minimum rather than noise.
---
### Conclusion:
The function is most likely an **exponentially scaled function with a single sharp minimum**, designed to test Bayesian optimization’s ability to handle **extreme landscapes** where most regions are vastly worse than the global optimum. The optimizer found the minimum early but continued exploring due to model uncertainty, leading to no further improvements.
